Formula Electric

Formula Electric at Berkeley · Aero Structures Engineer, aerodynamics team · Oct 2025 – present
Tools: SolidWorks, ANSYS Structural, topology optimization, carbon fiber layup, resin infusion

I design and structurally validate aerodynamic components for Berkeley's Formula SAE Electric car — currently focused on the front wing assembly, its mounting hardware, and the FEA work that keeps it light without giving up strength. This page is a running log of that work and will grow each season.

2025–26 season: front wing

The front wing generates a significant share of the car's downforce, but every gram in the mounting hardware is weight the car has to accelerate and brake against. My work this season centered on redesigning the mounting system — the pylons, U-channels, and ribs that connect the wing to the chassis — to cut mass while holding a team-wide minimum factor of safety of 1.75.

Design and mounting strategy

The wing mounts to the car only through the front element (FE1), removing the need for separate mounting ribs on the second element (FE2) and simplifying the load path. Mount height and the third element (FE3) are both adjustable, which lets the team tune center of pressure and upwash without a full redesign between events.

  • Pylons: 0.148 kg, a 41% mass reduction from the initial 250 g design, achieved through topology optimization. FOS 3.6.
  • Mounting U-channels: FOS 2.4.
  • Pylon mounting ribs: internal truss/spar system removed after optimization showed it wasn't load-bearing. FOS 3.2.

Structural analysis

Every component went through static structural FEA in ANSYS before manufacturing — checking von-Mises stress against the material's yield strength, then confirming the resulting factor of safety cleared the team's 1.75 minimum with margin.

The front-wing pylon and mounting parts were designed using load cases from the wing's aerodynamic downforce and drag, along with the car's maximum acceleration, then refined through topology optimization and repeated FEA. For the pylon, material was removed from the low-stress areas in the center to create the open triangular shape, while keeping more material around the upper wing mounts, lower chassis mounts, and center junction where the loads travel through. The mounting ribs were optimized the same way, including removing the internal truss/spar between the upper and lower rails while keeping the areas around the pylon mounts, vertical supports, and bolt holes reinforced. The final designs had minimum factors of safety of 4.02 for the pylon, 3.20 for the rib, and 2.99 for the mounting bracket, all above our target of 1.75.

Mesh convergence

Before this, structural simulations across the aero team were mostly treated as one-and-done runs, without mesh convergence or much validation of the model setup. I began looking into how to properly validate the FEA, testing element sizes from 5 mm down to 0.5 mm and checking when the results stopped changing significantly. This also led me to improve how bolts, contacts, and boundary conditions were modeled to reduce artificial stress singularities and fix issues in the simulations. I then implemented this validation process across the front-wing structural analysis so the results could be trusted for design decisions.

Assembly and detail

The pylon mounts directly to the wing ribs using M4 bolts, which were sized up from the M3 hardware used on SN4 because the smaller bolts were not secure enough. Three mounting holes in the pylon allow the wing to be raised to different heights for different aerodynamic configurations. The pylon attaches to the chassis with knurled thumbscrews, allowing the entire front wing to be removed in under a minute without tools. The stiffness tethers connect directly to a small tab on the pylon, so they stay tensioned and do not need to be adjusted when the wing is removed. Using a simple mounting tab also kept the design easy to manufacture and assemble.

Manufacturing

I completed several carbon fiber layups for the aero package, including bodywork side panels, the undertray, and the midplate and endplate sandwich panels. I worked with both resin infusion and wet layup processes, from preparing the fabric and core materials to laying up the parts, sealing the molds, and completing the cure. I also helped prepare and clean the molds before each layup to make sure the parts released properly and had a good surface finish.
